How physicians can choose a reputation or branding service

What each kind of provider does, what to check before you sign, the warning signs, and the rules physicians work within.

By Top Dynamos editorial team. Published November 6, 2026. About 7 minutes to read.

Who wrote this

Top Dynamos sells reputation management and publishing services. This guide does not rank providers, including us. It explains what to look for, so you can judge any provider yourself.

The short answer

Decide first what is wrong: wrong facts, too little about you, or one result with nothing beside it. Then choose the kind of provider that fixes that problem, and check that they record a dated baseline, show you every word before it goes out, follow patient privacy rules and put the monthly work in writing. Walk away from anyone who promises removals or rankings.

Eight things to check before you sign

Each one can be checked on a first call or in the contract.

  1. 1They start with a dated baseline

    Ask how they record what people find today, with the date and the exact searches, so change can be measured later.

  2. 2Every fact traces to a document

    Your license, board certification and affiliations should be checked against records, not copied from old profiles.

  3. 3You approve everything first

    Nothing should be published, sent or replied to in your name without your written approval.

  4. 4They know patient privacy rules

    Ask how they reply to reviews. The answer should never involve confirming that someone was your patient.

  5. 5They say what they cannot do

    A credible provider tells you which results are outside anyone's control before you pay.

  6. 6The work each month is written down

    For a monthly service, you should know the sites covered, the number of requests or pieces, and the report you will receive.

  7. 7Leaving is easy

    The terms, renewal and cancellation steps should be clear before you pay. Ask for cancelling to be as simple as signing up.

  8. 8You know who does the work

    Ask who writes, who checks, and who you can call. A name and a company address are basic.

Questions to ask on the first call

  • What will you record before you start, and will I get a copy?
  • Which sites and searches does the monthly work cover, and how many requests or pieces is that?
  • How do you reply to a negative review about a patient interaction?
  • What will you never do, even if I ask?
  • If I see no change in six months, what happens?
  • Is any award, listing or placement in your offer paid for, and is that disclosed to readers?
  • How do I cancel, and what do I keep afterward?

Warning signs

  • Promises to remove reviews or news articles on demand.
  • Promises of a position on Google or in AI answers.
  • Offers to write, buy or arrange reviews.
  • Review replies that mention a patient's visit, condition or treatment.
  • A paid award or ranking presented as an independent honor.
  • No written scope, no named contact, or a contract you cannot easily leave.

The rules physicians work within

A provider should know these without being told. This is general information, not legal advice.

  • Patient privacy

    The federal health privacy rule covers what a practice says in public replies to reviews. The HHS Office for Civil Rights has reached settlements with, and fined, practices that disclosed patient information online, including in replies to reviews.

  • Fake reviews

    Since October 21, 2024, a federal rule bans writing, buying or selling fake reviews, and offering rewards for reviews that express a particular sentiment.

  • Subscriptions

    A federal law on online subscriptions requires clear terms before billing, express informed consent and a simple way to stop recurring charges. Many states add their own automatic renewal rules.

  • Paid recognition

    Some awards for physicians are sold. A fee is not a problem in itself, but it should be disclosed, and eligibility should be clear. A health system's physician blog has warned that sales calls offering paid plaques or award listings are most likely scams.

Questions physicians ask

Do physicians need a reputation service at all?

Many do not. If a search for your name shows accurate profiles and nothing misleading, updating your own profiles once a year may be enough. A service makes sense when results are wrong, thin, confused with someone else, or dominated by one old item.

Can any company remove a bad review?

No outside company can remove a review on demand. A review that breaks a site's own rules can be reported, and the site decides. On Google, disagreeing with a review is not grounds for removal. Treat promises of removal as a warning sign.

How long before results change?

Profiles you control can often be updated within days. Other sites and search engines work on their own timing: Google says crawling alone can take from a few days to a few weeks, and wider changes often take months. A credible provider shows you the same searches before and after.

Is a paid award worth it?

It depends on what you want it for. Know that it is paid, check how eligibility is decided, and decide whether you are comfortable explaining it to patients and colleagues.
